## The Story of Complex Analysis

### 16th Century: The Birth of the Imaginary

Cardano met "square roots of negative numbers" while solving cubic equations. He called them "subtle and useless" — yet it was precisely these "useless" numbers that gave cubics complete solutions. Descartes later dubbed them "imaginary," and the name stuck.

### 18th Century: Euler Brings the Imaginary Mainstream

Euler linked $i$, $e$, and $\pi$, discovering $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. He also proved $i^i$ is real and systematically studied complex functions like $e^z$ and $\log z$. **Euler turned complex numbers from a "strange tool" into "full citizens of mathematics."**

### Early 19th Century: Cauchy's Foundation

Cauchy established the complete framework of complex function theory:

- The Cauchy–Riemann equations (the criterion for complex differentiability)
- Cauchy's integral theorem and integral formula
- The residue theorem

From then on, complex analysis became an independent, deep branch of mathematics.

### Mid 19th Century: Riemann's Geometric View

Riemann connected complex functions with geometry: conformal maps (angle-preserving maps), Riemann surfaces. He proved the Riemann mapping theorem — any simply connected region can be conformally mapped to the unit disk. **Complex analysis became deeply intertwined with geometry, topology, and algebra.**

### 20th Century to Today: From Pure Theory to Engineering

- The Fourier and Laplace transforms became the language of signal processing and control
- Complex analysis became the mathematical tool of quantum mechanics, fluid mechanics, and electromagnetism
- The Riemann hypothesis (the distribution of zeros of the $\zeta$ function) remains the deepest open problem in mathematics

> From "subtle and useless" square roots in cubic equations to the wavefunctions of quantum mechanics and the signal processing of the internet — the imaginary number completed a four-century comeback.
