## The Story of Topology

### 1736: Euler and the Königsberg Bridges

Citizens of Königsberg found they could not cross all seven bridges exactly once on a walk. Euler abstracted land to points and bridges to edges, proving the **necessary and sufficient condition for "one-stroke" drawing**: the number of odd-degree vertices must be 0 or 2. This was the shared origin of graph theory and topology — the first time "shape and connection" were liberated from measurement.

### Late 18th Century: Euler's Characteristic

Euler discovered $V-E+F=2$ and, through its generalizations, recognized that this number does not change under deformation. This "invariant" was the seed of topological thought: **certain quantities survive geometric deformation**.

### 19th Century: Riemann Surfaces and Multivalued Functions

To handle the "multivaluedness" of complex functions, Riemann invented Riemann surfaces — imagining the "layering" of function values as a surface. This forced mathematicians to take "the surface itself" seriously, rather than the space in which it is embedded. Riemann's ideas pushed topology from recreation to deep theory.

### Around 1900: Poincaré's Foundation

In his *Analysis Situs* papers, Poincaré formally founded algebraic topology: homotopy, homology, the fundamental group $\pi_1$. His **Poincaré conjecture** (every simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere) became one of the seven Millennium problems, proved by Perelman in 2003 using Ricci flow.

### 20th Century: From Analysis to Every Field

- **Point-set topology**: Hausdorff and Fréchet established axiomatic open-set theory, becoming the foundation of analysis
- **Algebraic topology**: homology theory, characteristic classes, Brouwer's fixed-point theorem — existence proofs for nonlinear equations depend on it
- **Applications explode**: data science (TDA), robotics, network science, and gauge theory in physics

> From the seven bridges of Königsberg to understanding the shape of the universe and the topology of data — topology turned "invariance under deformation" into a complete mathematical language.
